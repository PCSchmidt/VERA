## Literature review

The evidence on judging numeric and table-grounded claims is thin and mostly indirect, so this review separates what individual studies show from what they leave open. The Reassessing Extractive QA study reports that LLM-as-a-judge performs particularly well on number-related answers but faces challenges with more complex types, such as job titles [R5]. The same study reports that zero-shot, context-free judging often yields the best evaluation performance [R5]. These short-form extractive answers are not tabular claims requiring arithmetic, so the finding does not settle those conditions.

A study using graph-verified ground truth on financial documents reports a sharply different picture for structured tasks. The graph-verified financial-documents study reports that a grounded judge, given the full source document but no ground truth, achieved only 54.3% agreement, with a 33.1% false acceptance rate [R35]. In the graph-verified financial-documents study, a lenient judge inflates disagreement with the verified truth to 7.9%, and a blind judge without ground truth reaches 22.3% [R35]. The authors report that false acceptances concentrate in exact recall, multi-hop and threshold categories [R35]. Seeing the source therefore did not by itself make judging reliable in that setting, and access to ground truth mattered much more.

Work on the legal insurance domain also finds limits, though not on numeric claims. The authors of the Quebec insurance legal-QA judging study conclude that a single LLM judge is not trustworthy enough to evaluate high-stakes legal answers on its own [R6]. The Quebec insurance legal-QA judging study reports that selecting judges by their ability to catch errors, supplying an authoritative reference, and combining a few strong judges each reduces dangerous acceptances [R6].

Benchmarks for claim verification, which test models as verifiers rather than judges, indicate where table reasoning is hard. The SCITAB authors report that all models except GPT-4 achieved performance barely above random guessing on table-based scientific claims [R11]. The SCITAB authors also report that popular prompting techniques such as Chain-of-Thought do not achieve much performance gains on SCITAB [R11]. In an error analysis of program-of-thought predictions, the SCITAB authors categorized failures as grounding, ambiguity, calculation and program errors [R11]. The ClaimDB authors argue that at large scale, approaches that rely on reading the evidence break down, forcing a shift toward reasoning in executable programs [R120]. The Prolog study notes that chain-of-thought relies on an LLM to generate a sequence of arithmetic calculations which can be prone to cascaded calculation errors [R39].

General-purpose judge studies add background on reasoning limits and on agreement metrics. The MT-Bench authors present methods to address position bias and the limited grading ability for math questions [R20]. The construct-validity study reports that high judge agreement can coexist with weak sensitivity to changes in the construct being evaluated [R114]. The Survey on LLM-as-a-Judge says future work should fuse LLM judges with symbolic solvers or knowledge bases and build judges able to interpret figures and tables [R3].

Overall, the passages suggest that judge agreement is high for simple numeric answers checked against short context, and weak for multi-hop, threshold and structured retrieval claims, even with the source in view. What is not established is whether a judge equipped with a calculator, code execution or a recomputation step restores reliability on table-grounded arithmetic claims, since no supplied study tests a judge with such tools against programmatically verified labels. Also not established is how judge agreement varies with the number of reasoning steps in table claims, or whether results from financial documents, extractive QA and legal QA transfer to one another.

## Retrieval and its limits

This review rests on 12 search queries, which retrieved 120 candidate papers. A relevance screen kept 25 and set aside 95; 6 were read in full text and 19 at the abstract only. Where this review says that something is missing or not established, it means that these searches did not find it: it is not a measure of the literature, and no figure for how much of the field the searches missed is available for a topic given without a list of its key papers.
