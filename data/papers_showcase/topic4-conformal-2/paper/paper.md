# Coverage Guarantees and Assumptions of Conformal Prediction Under Distribution Shift: What Is Proved and Where the Proofs Stop

## Abstract

Conformal prediction gives finite-sample marginal coverage under exchangeability, but deployment data often break that assumption. This review asks what coverage each shift-oriented method actually proves and where the proofs stop. For weighted conformal prediction under covariate shift, the clean result is marginal coverage of at least 1 - alpha, and it is stated for exactly known likelihood ratios and a Y|X law that stays fixed. With estimated ratios, the sources support only weaker statements: coverage degrades with estimation error, an approximation term appears, and finite-sample validity generally needs extra conditions or doubly robust constructions. One training-conditional analysis is reported to extend to estimated ratios, while others report degradation or undercoverage. The claims do not say which coverage notions and conditions reconcile these. Label-shift variants with pseudo-label ratio estimates lose coverage as shift strengthens. Adaptive conformal inference differs in kind. It gives a long-run average coverage guarantee with no assumption on the data process, not a conditional or per-step guarantee. Worst-case f-divergence methods trade assumptions differently: they need exchangeable training data or a known divergence bound, and unrestricted conditional shift leaves only a [0,1] identified set. Open questions include simultaneous covariate and label shift with partially identifiable ratios, and how long-run coverage relates to local coverage under drift.

## Introduction

Conformal prediction converts almost any predictive algorithm into prediction intervals or sets with finite-sample marginal coverage under exchangeability [R3]. Deployment data, however, are rarely exchangeable with development data [R3]. Geographic transfer, changing case mix, sensor replacement, policy changes, and temporal drift can alter the covariate distribution P X , the conditional outcome law P Y |X , or both [R3]. One source describes the difficulty for covariate shift directly: exchangeability of calibration and test points fails, which makes the standard coverage argument void [R4].

Weighted conformal prediction extends validity to covariate shift by weighting calibration scores with the density ratio [R3]. A review of Tibshirani et al. describes the method as designed for covariate shift where the likelihood ratio between training and test covariate distributions is known [R2]. Gibbs and Candès describe Tibshirani et al.'s reweighting approach as requiring the conditional distribution of Y|X to be constant between training and testing and the likelihood ratio to be either known or very accurately estimated [R8]. A further source states that when likelihood ratios must be estimated, coverage degrades at a rate controlled by the estimation error [R4].

Adaptive conformal inference is presented as achieving the desired coverage frequency over long-time intervals irrespective of the true data generating process [R8]. The same paper describes Cauchois et al. as developing prediction sets valid whenever the two distributions are close in f-divergence [R8].

When likelihood ratios must be estimated, coverage is reported to degrade at a rate controlled by the estimation error, and for estimated weighted conformal prediction the bound inherits an approximation term and is not relabeled exact. Simultaneous covariate and label shift, with partially identifiable likelihood ratios, is listed as an open challenge. One source reports that, for estimated weighted conformal prediction, the bound inherits an approximation term and is not relabeled exact [R3]. Another lists simultaneous covariate and label shift, with partially identifiable likelihood ratios, as an open challenge [R4].

## Weighted conformal prediction under covariate shift: guarantees with known versus estimated likelihood ratios

This section reviews what the supplied sources establish about weighted conformal prediction under covariate shift when the likelihood ratio is known and when it is estimated. The original work states that a weighted version of conformal prediction yields distribution-free prediction intervals when the likelihood ratio between test and training covariate distributions is known, or can be estimated accurately with a large set of unlabeled test covariates [R29]. A later tabular-regression paper restates the known-ratio result as marginal coverage of at least 1 - alpha under covariate shift, provided the likelihood ratios are exactly known [R4]. The same paper says that when ratios must be estimated, coverage degrades at a rate controlled by the estimation error [R4]. The adaptive conformal inference paper adds that reweighting requires Y|X to be constant between training and testing and the ratio to be known or very accurately estimated [R8]. The DriftGuard audit paper says that with estimated ratios, finite-sample validity generally needs additional conditions or doubly robust constructions [R3]. The DriftGuard paper (R3) separates oracle from estimated weighting: it states that oracle test-point weighted conformal prediction has its stated finite-sample guarantee, while estimated weighted conformal prediction inherits an approximation term and is not relabeled "exact." [R3]. A weight-clipping study reports that weighted conformal prediction can suffer significant undercoverage when the density ratio is unbounded or must be learned [R32]. A training-conditional analysis reports that its treatment extends to estimated likelihood ratios [R67]. The sources agree that known ratios give the clean guarantee and that estimation error weakens it, but they differ on how far estimated-ratio results can be pushed toward finite-sample statements. The tabular-regression paper lists simultaneous covariate and label shift, with partially identifiable ratios, as an open challenge [R4].

## Label shift extensions and the role of estimated density ratios

The label-shift classification study examines reweighting of conformal and calibration procedures whenever some unlabeled data from the target distribution is available [R153]. The Bayesian-score paper reports that, under stronger shift, all methods incur some coverage loss due to pseudo-label-based density-ratio estimation [R84]. The conformal Bayes study states that its two approaches restore nominal target-domain coverage through importance-weighted conformal calibration [R111].

## Beyond exchangeability: weighted quantiles and randomization (Barber et al.)

Barber and coauthors state that conformal prediction's validity relies on exchangeability of the data and symmetry of the model fitting algorithm [R12]. The paper introduces weighted quantiles to provide robustness against distribution drift [R12]. The authors claim substantially less loss of coverage under drift, while matching existing coverage guarantees when the data are exchangeable [R12].

A covariate-shift paper that cites this work states that weighted conformal prediction gives marginal coverage of at least 1 - alpha when the likelihood ratios are exactly known [R4]. The same paper says coverage degrades at a rate controlled by the estimation error when the ratios must be estimated, and attributes this to Barber et al [R4]. The auditing paper takes a similar position, saying that estimated weighted conformal prediction inherits an approximation term and is not relabeled exact [R3]. The Wasserstein paper notes that prior studies bound the coverage gap with total variation distance, which it says cannot identify how the gap changes under distribution shift at a given alpha [R120].

Several statements bear on how weighting is used to handle departures from exchangeability: one account ties conformal validity to exchangeability and symmetric model fitting, and another reports that weighted conformal prediction yields marginal coverage of at least 1 - alpha when likelihood ratios are exactly known but degrades with estimation error when they must be estimated.

## Adaptive conformal inference for temporal drift: long-run coverage without data-process assumptions

The next question is what guarantee adaptive methods for temporal drift provide, and under what conditions. Gibbs and Candès state that, while previous conformal inference methods rely on the assumption that the data points are exchangeable, their adaptive approach provably achieves the desired coverage frequency over long-time intervals irrespective of the true data generating process [R8]. The ACI authors describe their Proposition 4.1 as an agnostic guarantee of correct long-term empirical coverage frequency and as an approximately tight bound on worst-case behaviour right after initialization [R8]. The same paper says that, when the distribution shift is small and the prediction algorithm takes a certain simple form, ACI additionally obtains approximate marginal coverage at most time steps [R8].

Subsequent work builds on this line of research, and the following passages turn to it. The abstract of the follow-up work by Gibbs and Candès states that ACI requires knowledge of the rate of change of the data-generating mechanism [R11]. Error-quantified Conformal Inference establishes a long-term coverage guarantee under arbitrary dependence and distribution shift [R57]. A dropout-informed variant reports that its long-run coverage guarantee is score-agnostic, inherited from ACI itself rather than a new property of studentization [R28]. Another study notes that ACI only adapts the quantile threshold and cannot shift the interval center [R74].

## Robust conformal methods under f-divergence and worst-case shift

This section turns to conformal methods built for worst-case shifts, and to what their proofs cover. The robust validation work presents a method whose prediction sets give almost exactly the right coverage level for any test distribution in an f-divergence ball around the training population [R139]. Robust validation achieves nearly valid coverage in finite samples, under only the condition that the training data be exchangeable [R139]. Robust validation also estimates the amount of expected future shift, and its authors prove consistency of the estimators [R139].

The fine-grained framework splits the problem differently, reweighting training samples for an identifiable covariate shift while protecting against worst-case conditional shift bounded in an f-divergence ball [R23].

Adaptive conformal inference is described by its authors as an agnostic guarantee of correct long-term empirical coverage frequency irrespective of the true data generating process [R8]. Gibbs and Candès describe the f-divergence method of Cauchois et al. as forming prediction sets that are valid whenever P 1 and P 0 are close in f -divergence [R8]. A robust conformal runtime-verification study assumes a known upper bound on the f-divergence between deployment and design distributions [R51]. The sources differ in what they require. The ball-based robust validation method relies on exchangeable training data and covers test distributions within an f-divergence ball. A runtime-verification study of robust conformal prediction assumes a known upper bound on the f-divergence between deployment and design distributions. Adaptive conformal inference is described by its authors as an agnostic guarantee of long-term empirical coverage frequency irrespective of the true data generating process. The fine-grained framework instead reweights for an identifiable covariate shift while protecting against worst-case conditional shift in an f-divergence ball.

## Gaps in the proofs: estimated weights, mixed shifts, and unidentifiable conditional shift

The next passages turn to what happens to coverage guarantees once the weights must be estimated rather than known. One audit paper states that when density ratios are estimated, finite-sample validity generally needs additional conditions or doubly robust constructions [R3]. A study of training-conditional coverage reports that its analysis extends to estimated likelihood ratios [R67]. A tabular-regression paper reports adaptive prediction sets that achieve near-optimal conditional coverage rates whenever the likelihood ratio is estimable at a sufficient rate [R26]. A label-shift Bayesian score paper reports coverage loss under stronger shift because density ratios are estimated from pseudo-labels [R84]. The sources differ on estimated weights: one states that finite-sample validity generally needs additional conditions or doubly robust constructions, another reports that its training-conditional coverage analysis extends to estimated likelihood ratios, and a third reports near-optimal conditional coverage rates whenever the likelihood ratio is estimable at a sufficient rate. A further question is how these guarantees fare when covariate and label shift occur together. A tabular-regression paper on covariate shift lists extending the framework to simultaneous covariate and label shift with partially identifiable likelihood ratios as an open challenge [R4]. The audit paper establishes a sharp [0,1] identified set for target coverage under unrestricted conditional shift [R3]. A fine-grained robust method handles an identifiable covariate shift while protecting against worst-case conditional shift bounded in an f-divergence ball [R23].

## Discussion

*Interpretation: VERA's reading of the claims named after each statement, judged to follow from them; not checked against quotes.*

The claims agree that standard conformal validity rests on exchangeability, and that deployment data often break it, which motivates shift-specific methods (C1, C2, C4, C25). For weighted conformal prediction under covariate shift, the sources agree that the clean guarantee is tied to a known likelihood ratio: marginal coverage of at least 1 - alpha when the ratios are exactly known (C6, C13, C14, C28). The sources also agree that estimated ratios weaken this guarantee: coverage degrades with estimation error, the bound carries an approximation term, and finite-sample validity generally needs extra conditions or doubly robust constructions (C8, C11, C15, C17, C18, C46). Reweighting is also described as requiring Y|X to be constant between training and testing, so the covariate-shift guarantee does not cover a change in the conditional law (C7, C16). Adaptive conformal inference differs in kind from the weighting methods: its authors present it as provably achieving the desired coverage frequency over long-time intervals irrespective of the true data generating process, and describe this as an agnostic guarantee of long-term empirical coverage frequency (a long-run average, not a per-step guarantee). Only when the shift is small and the prediction algorithm takes a certain simple form does it additionally obtain approximate marginal coverage at most time steps. By contrast, the weighting methods require the likelihood ratio to be known or very accurately estimated (C9, C32, C33, C34, C43, C7). The sources diverge on whether estimated weights are covered. One reports that a training-conditional analysis extends to estimated ratios, while others report degradation or undercoverage, so the results are not in conflict only if they concern different coverage notions or conditions. The claims do not settle which (C19, C20, C47, C48, C15). Worst-case methods trade assumptions differently. Robust validation reports nearly valid finite-sample coverage over an f-divergence ball under exchangeable training data, while another study assumes a known divergence bound. Another source reports a [0,1] identified set under unrestricted conditional shift (C39, C40, C45, C51).

## Conclusions

*Interpretation: VERA's reading of the claims named after each statement, judged to follow from them; not checked against quotes.*

Weighted conformal prediction has a proved marginal lower bound of 1 - alpha, but the claims state it only for known likelihood ratios and a fixed Y|X (C14, C16, C28). With estimated weights the claims support only a weaker statement: coverage degrades with estimation error or an approximation term enters, and the claims do not give a general exact guarantee (C8, C11, C17). Adaptive conformal inference provides a long-run average coverage guarantee without assumptions on the data process, not a conditional or per-step finite-sample guarantee (C9, C33, C34).

## A research agenda

*Interpretation: VERA's reading of the claims named after each statement, judged to follow from them; not checked against quotes.*

Compare coverage of known-weight and estimated-weight conformal prediction as the estimation error is varied, to test the claimed degradation rate and clarify the approximation term (C8, C11, C19). Reconcile the training-conditional extension to estimated ratios with the reports of degradation by stating which coverage notion and conditions each result uses (C20, C47, C15, C17). Study label-shift reweighting where density ratios come from pseudo-labels, measuring coverage loss as shift strengthens (C22, C23, C49). Develop and test methods for simultaneous covariate and label shift with partially identifiable ratios, including the partial-identification limits (C12, C21, C50). Evaluate in the same experiments the fine-grained approach, which pairs identifiable covariate shift with an f-divergence ball on the conditional shift, against pure weighting and pure divergence methods, and check how the unrestricted-shift [0,1] set bounds them (C42, C52, C51). Measure how ACI's long-run guarantee relates to local coverage under drift, given its limited adaptation of the interval center (C33, C34, C38).
