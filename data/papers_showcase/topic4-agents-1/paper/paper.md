# How Automated Research Agents Are Judged: Operational Measures of Correctness, Reproducibility, and Novelty, and Their Validity Weaknesses

## Abstract

This review examines how published benchmarks and studies of automated research agents operationalize correctness, reproducibility, and novelty, and where those measures are weak. For correctness and reproducibility, CORE-Bench counts a task as correct when all reported answers fall within a 95% prediction interval of three manual runs. PaperBench instead uses hierarchical manual rubrics that give partial credit and are graded by an LLM-based judge. That judge was motivated by the tens of hours that expert grading of one attempt can take. Its reported F1 of 0.83 is called a reasonable stand-in, yet its authors say more work is needed on judge accuracy. SciReplicate-Bench criticizes reliance on manual criteria and LLM judgments and prioritizes execution accuracy. For novelty, LLM judging appears fragile: judges rate generated questions as highly novel, and prompt framing can flip verdicts on over half of identical pairs. Grounding in retrieved papers or future publications is proposed, but the evidence does not show which approach is valid. Run-to-run variance is reported, while single-run scoring is typical. Date windows and periodic updates address contamination, though paraphrasing can bypass n-gram decontamination. The evidence does not show whether these benchmarks are contaminated, or how much threshold outcomes shift. Open work includes chance-corrected judge validation, repeated runs, direct contamination measurement, and construct-validity checks with more capable agents.

## Introduction

The RQ-Bench study states in its introduction that LLMs are now used not only to generate scientific ideas, but also to judge them [R2]. The same study notes that if LLMs generate a scientific artifact and other LLMs judge its novelty, the whole pipeline depends on whether LLM-as-judge is reliable [R2].

Reproducibility benchmarks form one strand of this work. CORE-Bench asks whether AI agents can automate computational reproducibility of published scientific research [R11]. The CORE-Bench authors decide that an agent solves a task correctly if its reported results for all questions fall within a 95% prediction interval of the results from three manual runs [R11]. The CORE-Bench authors suggest that building the benchmark on public repositories enables periodic updates, which could mitigate concerns about contamination and saturation [R11].

PaperBench grades replication attempts with rubrics that hierarchically decompose each task into smaller sub-tasks with clear grading criteria, and its authors report that grading a single attempt can take a human expert tens of hours given the complexity of ML research papers. PaperBench develops rubrics that hierarchically decompose each replication task into smaller sub-tasks with clear grading criteria [R24]. The PaperBench authors report that, given the complexity of ML research papers, grading a single replication attempt can take tens of hours for a human expert [R24].

Other concerns about validity come from outside the research-agent literature. The survey on benchmark data contamination defines it as occurring when language models inadvertently incorporate evaluation benchmark information from their training data [R10]. A clinical-agent study observes that benchmarks typically score one run per task and rarely ask whether identical inputs produce identical actions [R27].

## Method

The question this paper answers was proposed by VERA from the user's topic and confirmed by the user: In published benchmarks and studies of automated research agents (for example MLE-bench, PaperBench, SciReplicate-Bench, CORE-Bench, and LLM-judged idea-novelty evaluations), which operational measures are used to decide whether an agent's output is correct, reproducible, or novel, and what validity weaknesses does each measure have, such as reliance on LLM judges, rubric leniency, test-set contamination, medal or threshold cutoffs that ignore variance across runs, and novelty scored without checking against the prior literature?

Search. 12 queries were sent to the paper-search services: “MLE-bench machine learning engineering agents benchmark”; “PaperBench replicating AI research agents”; “SciReplicate-Bench reproducing algorithms from papers”; “CORE-Bench computational reproducibility agent benchmark”; “LLM-judged novelty of research ideas evaluation”; “LLM-as-a-judge reliability bias evaluation”; “rubric-based grading of paper replication”; “benchmark data contamination in language model evaluation”; “variance across runs agent evaluation statistical reliability”; “survey of AI scientist automated scientific discovery agents”; “novelty assessment against prior literature retrieval”; “limitations and failure modes of research agent benchmarks”. They retrieved 120 candidate papers. A relevance screen, judged on titles and abstracts, kept 41 and set aside 79.

Reading. Every kept paper was read at its abstract; 7 were also read in full text, and 34 at the abstract only. This gave 107 evidence passages, the only text the paper may quote.

Writing and checking. The evidence sections were drafted sentence by sentence; every sentence that attributes something to a source carries a quote copied from one of those passages. Each quote was checked to be verbatim in its source, and a judge other than the writer checked that the passage supports the sentence and that the quote alone states it; sentences that failed were repaired once or removed. 49 claims from 20 sources survived, resting on 42 distinct quotes (a quote used by more than one sentence is one piece of evidence). Sentences without a claim were checked too: a synthesis names the claims it rests on and was judged to follow from them; any other sentence was judged to state no fact. Interpretation sections, where present, rest only on those claims and are labelled as such.

Models. Written by anthropic/claude-sonnet-5.5; judged by anthropic/claude-sonnet-5.5, z-ai/glm-5.3-flash.

## LLM-as-judge reliability and bias in grading agent outputs

The sources reviewed here bear on how far LLM judges can be trusted to grade agent outputs, starting with the judge used in PaperBench. PaperBench's authors report that their best LLM-based judge, which uses o3-mini-high with custom scaffolding, achieves an F1 score of 0.83 on the auxiliary evaluation, suggesting it is a reasonable stand-in for a human judge [R24]. The PaperBench authors state that more work is needed to improve judge accuracy and understand judges' strengths and weaknesses [R24].

A large-scale evaluation found that judge validation relying on exact-match agreement does not correct for chance and systematically overstates discriminative ability [R62]. The PaperBench authors report an F1 of 0.83 for their best judge and call it a reasonable stand-in for a human judge, while also stating that more work is needed to understand judges' strengths and weaknesses. A large-scale evaluation found that validating judges by exact-match agreement does not correct for chance and overstates discriminative ability. Together, these sources suggest that a single agreement score may not capture how well a judge discriminates.

Turning to novelty judging, the evidence on judge validity needs separate consideration. A controlled study of novelty judges found that telling the judge that reviewers found one idea novel and the other not can change its verdict on more than half of identical idea pairs [R58]. HindSight reports that its scores are negatively correlated with LLM-judged novelty [R87].

PaperBench pairs each paper with manually created, hierarchical rubrics that allow partial progress toward replication to be measured at a fine grain, and its authors report that its best LLM-based judge reaches an F1 score of 0.83 on the auxiliary evaluation. PaperBench pairs each paper with manually created rubrics that specify the outcomes needed for replication [R24]. PaperBench rubrics are hierarchical, so that partial progress toward replication can be measured at a fine grain [R24]. The PaperBench authors report that grading a single replication attempt can take tens of hours for a human expert [R24]. The PaperBench authors report that their best LLM-based judge, which uses o3-mini-high with custom scaffolding, achieves an F1 score of 0.83 on the auxiliary evaluation [R24].

SciReplicate-Bench criticizes this design, saying PaperBench-style approaches depend on manual criteria and LLM judgments [R28]. CORE-Bench takes another route, scoring an agent as correct when its answers fall within a 95% prediction interval of three manual runs [R11]. A later CORE-Bench study reports surfacing threats to construct validity in CORE-Bench Hard that are difficult to anticipate with less capable agents [R52].

The PaperBench authors acknowledge that judge accuracy on large, complex outputs needs more work [R24]. A survey of agentic evaluation papers reports that about 82% of its sample use structurally mismatched, incomplete, or absent inter-rater reliability metrics [R55].

## Test-set contamination and data leakage

The TRUCE authors state that the majority of open source benchmarks available today have been contaminated or leaked into LLMs [R154]. The rephrased-samples study reports that simple variations of test data, such as paraphrasing and translation, can bypass n-gram decontamination [R63].

Several research-agent benchmarks, namely SciReplicate-Bench, ResearchBench and CORE-Bench, rely on publication date or public repositories as their safeguard against contamination, as the sources report. SciReplicate-Bench selected 2024 papers with public code to mitigate leakage, ResearchBench restricts itself to 2024-onward publications to minimize overlap with pretraining data, and CORE-Bench says its public-repository foundation allows periodic updates that could mitigate contamination. SciReplicate-Bench selected papers published in 2024 with public code repositories to mitigate the risk of data leakage [R28]. ResearchBench states that, to prevent data contamination, it focuses exclusively on publications from 2024 onward, which it says ensures minimal overlap with LLM pretraining data [R136]. CORE-Bench states that its foundation in public repositories enables periodic updates that could mitigate concerns about contamination and saturation [R11].

The next passages turn to evidence of memorization and to alternatives to public test sets. The tabular-data study finds that LLMs perform better on datasets seen during training, indicating that memorization leads to overfitting [R150]. The open-source contamination report finds that data contamination does not necessarily lead to increased model metrics [R76]. TRUCE proposes keeping test datasets private and evaluating models without revealing the test data to the model [R154].

## Run-to-run variance and threshold or medal cutoffs

Evidence on run-to-run variance in agent evaluation comes from several benchmarks, which report high variance across runs and recommend repeating evaluations. A related rerun study of LLM judging found single-trial judging to be too noisy for high-stakes use. PaperBench reports that for most agents there is high variance in results on the same paper, and its authors recommend using several seeds when evaluating [R24]. FIRE-Bench reports that even its strongest agents achieve limited rediscovery success and exhibit high variance across runs [R113]. The two benchmark papers therefore both report high run-to-run variance for research agents. The same study concludes that single-trial LLM judging is often too noisy for high-stakes evaluation [R30]. In a clinical agent benchmark, a rerun study found that the benchmark reports the same failing verdict for materially different behaviour in 22 of 43 divergent groups [R27]. Taken together, these sources point toward repeated runs rather than a single run as the basis for any pass or fail verdict.

## Novelty evaluation: LLM judges, human judgment, and prior-literature grounding

Novelty assessment has been approached through several routes, including human expert judgment, LLM judges, grounding in retrieved real papers, and scoring against later publications, and the sources reflect different findings about how far each can be trusted. The human study with over 100 NLP researchers reports that LLM-generated ideas were judged more novel than human expert ideas while being judged slightly weaker on feasibility [R107]. The same study acknowledges that human novelty judgments are hard [R107]. RQ-Bench reports that LLM judges rate model-generated research questions as highly novel, a pattern its authors call a novelty mirage [R2]. A study of novelty-judge design found that simply telling the judge that reviewers found one idea novel and the other not can change its verdict on more than half of the identical idea pairs it is shown, shifting pairwise accuracy by over 50 points and occasionally pushing it below chance [R58]. The study of novelty-judge design reported that retrieval and larger reasoning budgets help little, and that two purpose-built novelty evaluators are outperformed by its cheapest prompted baseline [R58]. OpenNovelty takes a grounding approach, tying novelty assessments to retrieved real papers [R59]. HindSight instead scores ideas against future publications, and it found HindSight scores negatively correlated with LLM-judged novelty [R87].

## Discussion

*Interpretation: VERA's reading of the claims named after each statement, judged to follow from them; not checked against quotes.*

The claims show benchmarks using different operational measures of correctness: CORE-Bench counts a task correct when all reported answers fall within a 95% prediction interval of three manual runs, while PaperBench uses hierarchical manual rubrics for partial credit graded by an LLM-based judge (C4, C6, C22, C23, C25). Rubric-based grading is motivated by cost: PaperBench reports that expert grading of one attempt can take tens of hours, which led to an automated judge. That judge's reported F1 of 0.83 is described as a reasonable stand-in, but the same authors say more work is needed on judge accuracy (C13, C17, C18, C29). The claims pull apart on whether judges can be trusted. PaperBench presents its judge as reasonable, whereas SciReplicate-Bench criticizes reliance on manual criteria and LLM judgments and prioritizes execution accuracy (C15, C26, C17). For novelty, the claims point to fragility in LLM judging: the pipeline depends on judge reliability, judges rate generated questions as highly novel, and prompt framing can flip verdicts on over half of identical pairs (C2, C45, C20, C46). Grounding in outside evidence is proposed as an alternative: tying novelty assessments to retrieved papers, or scoring against future publications, which was found negatively correlated with LLM-judged novelty. The claims do not show which approach is valid (C48, C49, C21, C47). Variance across runs is a recurring concern: PaperBench reports high variance on the same paper and recommends several seeds, FIRE-Bench reports high run-to-run variance, and a clinical study notes that benchmarks usually score one run per task (C39, C40, C9). Contamination is handled by date windows (2024 onward) and by periodic updates from public repositories, yet other claims say paraphrasing can bypass n-gram decontamination and that many open benchmarks are reported as contaminated (C33, C34, C35, C32, C31).

## Conclusions

*Interpretation: VERA's reading of the claims named after each statement, judged to follow from them; not checked against quotes.*

The studies use several operational measures: CORE-Bench counts a task as correct when all reported results fall within a 95% prediction interval of three manual runs; PaperBench uses hierarchical rubrics that decompose replication into sub-tasks; SciReplicate-Bench prioritizes execution accuracy and criticizes PaperBench-style approaches for relying on manual criteria and LLM judgments; and OpenNovelty grounds novelty assessments in retrieved real papers. The claims name a weakness for only some of these, such as the reliance on manual criteria and LLM judgments that SciReplicate-Bench attributes to PaperBench-style grading (C4, C6, C15, C48). The reliability of LLM judges is an acknowledged open problem. The PaperBench authors say more work is needed to improve judge accuracy and to understand judges' strengths and weaknesses, and a separate study concludes that single-trial LLM judging is often too noisy for high-stakes evaluation (C18, C41). The claims support concern about threshold or single-run scoring, since variance across runs is reported, but they do not quantify how much medal or threshold outcomes change (C39, C40, C9). Contamination mitigations are proposed, but the claims do not show that any of these benchmarks is or is not contaminated, and one report finds contamination does not necessarily raise metrics (C16, C35, C37).

## A research agenda

*Interpretation: VERA's reading of the claims named after each statement, judged to follow from them; not checked against quotes.*

Benchmarks typically score one run per task, and PaperBench reports high variance on the same paper for most agents and recommends several seeds. FIRE-Bench likewise reports high variance across runs. This supports repeating evaluations several times per task to see how often outcomes change. CORE-Bench's pass criterion is defined against three manual runs, and the claims do not say it was rerun (C9, C39, C40, C4). Validate rubric judges with chance-corrected agreement rather than exact match, and report reliability metrics, given criticism of exact-match validation and missing reliability metrics (C19, C30, C25). Test novelty judges for sensitivity to prompt framing and compare ungrounded, retrieval-grounded, and future-impact scoring on the same ideas (C20, C47, C48, C49). Measure contamination directly in date-windowed benchmarks, including paraphrase-robust checks, and test whether private or periodically updated test sets change results (C33, C34, C32, C38, C35). Examine construct validity in benchmarks with more capable agents, as a later case study found threats hard to anticipate earlier (C12).

## Threats to validity

Coverage. The paper rests on what 12 queries found; the search was not repeated for the paper. A finding reported as missing means these searches did not find it, not that it does not exist.

Depth of reading. 34 of 41 kept papers were read at the abstract only, so their methods and caveats are known only as their abstracts state them.

What the checks cover. Each claim was checked against the passage it quotes, not against the whole source or the wider literature. A claim can be supported by its quote and still leave out a qualification stated elsewhere in the paper it cites. Interpretation sections were judged to follow from the claims they name; they were not checked against the sources.

Same-family grading. The writer is a Claude model; the judges are a different model family, and any independent scoring of this paper by a Claude model shares the writer's family.
