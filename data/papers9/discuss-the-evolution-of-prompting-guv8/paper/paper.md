# From Prompts to Harnesses: How Prompt, Context, and Agentic Harness Engineering Are Defined and What Components Each Stage Is Credited With

## Abstract

This review traces how prompt engineering, context engineering, and harness engineering are defined in recent academic and practitioner literature, and which components each stage is credited with. Prompt engineering is defined consistently as designing instructions to guide model output without altering parameters, with one source also framing prompts as a form of programming. Yet the field is described as having conflicting terminology and a fragmented understanding of effective prompts. Context engineering is framed as a shift from input-level linguistic control to system-level orchestration, and as adapting inputs with instructions, strategies, or evidence instead of weight updates; it is tied to concrete concerns such as exceeding the usable window and degraded reasoning and tool-calling reliability. The harness is described as the external execution structure around the model, and a layered account emerges: instructions, then evidence and history, then the operating environment. Retrieval, memory, tool use, orchestration, and verification loops are attributed mostly to harness and agentic layers, though not exclusively. Boundaries remain unsettled, since sources offer both nesting and separation views. Evaluation evidence shows that harness changes can reverse model rankings. No single architecture is shown to be best. Open work includes comparing boundary definitions on a shared corpus, standardised harness reporting, realistic context-management tests, and cross-model validation.

## Introduction

Prompt engineering is described in one survey as a technique that strategically designs task-specific instructions, referred to as prompts, to guide model output without altering parameters [R1]. One paper describes prompts as instructions given to an LLM to enforce rules, automate processes, and ensure specific qualities (and quantities) of generated output [R11]. The same paper adds that prompts are also a form of programming that can customize the outputs and interactions with an LLM [R11]. The problem this review addresses begins with how that early framing has been extended and, in places, questioned. Context management has also become a concrete concern for agents: one study notes that LLM agents on long-running tasks inevitably exceed their usable context window, forcing a choice about what history to discard [R18]. The agentic variant is said to embed autonomous AI agents into the RAG pipeline [R17]. Attention then turns to the harness, the term used for the execution system around a model. One survey reports that changing the execution system around a fixed model can alter performance and even reverse model rankings [R6]. Another paper defines scaling the harness as treating the structured execution layer around a foundation model as a first-class object of design, evaluation, and optimization [R26]. A separate survey states that it takes the agent loop, not the model in isolation, as its unit of analysis [R8].

## Method

The question this paper answers was proposed by VERA from the user's topic and confirmed by the user: Between 2025 and the present, how have the definitions, scope, and claimed boundaries of prompt engineering, context engineering, harnesses, and agentic harnesses been articulated in the academic and practitioner literature, and which technical components (e.g., retrieval, memory, tool use, orchestration, evaluation loops) were attributed to each stage?

Search. 12 queries were sent to the paper-search services: “prompt engineering to context engineering definition”; “context engineering for large language model agents”; “agent harness design for LLM agents”; “agentic harness orchestration tool use memory”; “survey of context engineering techniques”; “retrieval-augmented generation and agent memory architectures”; “in-context learning and prompt design foundations”; “LLM agent scaffolding and tool-calling frameworks”; “benchmarks for evaluating LLM agent harnesses”; “long-context limitations context rot degradation”; “failure modes of autonomous LLM agents”; “survey of prompt engineering methods taxonomy”. They retrieved 120 candidate papers. A relevance screen, judged on titles and abstracts, kept 46 and set aside 74.

Reading. Every kept paper was read at its abstract; 15 were also read in full text, and 31 at the abstract only. This gave 195 evidence passages, the only text the paper may quote.

Writing and checking. The evidence sections were drafted sentence by sentence; every sentence that attributes something to a source carries a quote copied from one of those passages. Each quote was checked to be verbatim in its source, and a judge other than the writer checked that the passage supports the sentence and that the quote alone states it; sentences that failed were repaired once or removed. 57 claims from 21 sources survived, resting on 47 distinct quotes (a quote used by more than one sentence is one piece of evidence). Sentences without a claim were checked too: a synthesis names the claims it rests on and was judged to follow from them; any other sentence was judged to state no fact. Interpretation sections, where present, rest only on those claims and are labelled as such.

Models. Written by anthropic/claude-sonnet-5.5; judged by anthropic/claude-sonnet-5.5, z-ai/glm-5.3-flash.

## Prompt engineering: definitions, scope, and terminological fragmentation

The following passages turn from how prompt engineering is defined to how its terminology is described. One survey states that prompt engineering involves strategically designing task-specific instructions, referred to as prompts, to guide model output without altering parameters [R1]. A separate wireless-networks overview defines the perspective as designing prompts to guide LLMs to generate desired output without updating LLM parameters [R141]. The prompt pattern catalog frames prompts as a form of programming that can customize the outputs and interactions with an LLM [R11]. One survey and a separate wireless-networks overview both describe prompt design as guiding model output without altering or updating the model's parameters, and the prompt pattern catalog frames prompts as a form of programming that customizes a model's outputs and interactions. The Prompt Report describes the field as suffering from conflicting terminology and a fragmented ontological understanding of what constitutes an effective prompt [R48]. The Prompt Report, a systematic survey of prompt engineering techniques, presents a vocabulary of 33 terms and a taxonomy of 58 LLM prompting techniques [R48]. A practitioner-focused study calls prompt engineering a critical yet loosely defined skill [R39]. The Prompt Report's authors adopt an observational stance and make no claims to the validity of the presented techniques [R48]. The boundary between prompt engineering and later stages is taken up in the sections that follow.

## Context engineering: moving from prompt text to system-level context

Context engineering is framed as a move away from writing prompt text and toward managing the system around the model, and the sources below draw this contrast in different ways. The ACE authors describe context adaptation as modifying inputs with instructions, strategies, or evidence rather than weight updates [R94]. The ACE framework treats contexts as evolving playbooks that accumulate, refine, and organize strategies through generation, reflection, and curation [R94]. The survey in R2 frames the field as a shift from input-level linguistic control to system-level contextual orchestration, and it names context dilution and computational overhead as persistent limitations [R2]. The Code as Agent Harness paper draws the boundary by saying that prompt engineering changes instructions while context engineering changes what evidence is presented to the model [R49]. The externalization review calls the harness's separation of the prompt into layers a structured form of context engineering [R87]. The harness-scaling paper lists a context constructor as one of six components of an agent [R26]. Context degradation is named as a problem for context-managed systems in two ways: as a persistent limitation of context engineering (context dilution), and as a progressive decline in reasoning and tool-calling reliability as the context window fills (context rot). R20 describes context rot as the progressive and nonlinear degradation of agent reasoning quality and tool calling reliability as the context window fills during extended coding sessions [R20]. In a forced-compression experiment, summarize-oldest reached 94.2% recall, keep-most-relevant 60.0%, and drop-oldest 41.7% [R18]. The R18 authors acknowledge that their setup is synthetic, single-model, and single-budget [R18].

## Harness engineering: the structure built around a fixed model

The survey on harness engineering for LLM agents understands the agent harness as the external execution support structure built around the model [R29]. The scaling-the-harness paper says an agent is not simply a model with a prompt, but a system of six interacting components, including a memory store, a context constructor, a skill-routing layer, an orchestration loop, and a verification and governance layer [R26]. The externalization review assigns the roles differently: memory externalizes state across time, skills externalize procedural expertise, protocols externalize interaction structure, and harness engineering unifies them into governed execution [R87].

Two sources place the harness in relation to the model and its surrounding practices, and they describe it differently: one treats it as the external execution support structure built around the model, while the other describes an agent as a system of six interacting components rather than a model with a prompt, including a memory store, a context constructor, a skill-routing layer, an orchestration loop, and a verification and governance layer. The Agentic Harness Engineering section states that prompt engineering changes instructions and context engineering changes what evidence is presented, whereas harness engineering treats the operating environment itself as the object of analysis [R49]. The externalization review describes the harness's layered model boundary as a structured form of context engineering [R87].

The next question is how the sources separate harness engineering from prompt and context engineering. The harness-aware evaluation survey reports that changing the execution system around a fixed model can alter performance and even reverse model rankings [R6]. The scaling-the-harness paper cites SWE-agent as showing that careful tool-schema design alone can improve benchmark accuracy substantially even with a fixed backbone model [R26]. The harness-engineering survey also describes a shifting boundary in which routine capability-bearing support migrates into model weights while constraint-bearing governance functions remain external [R29]. Two sources differ in how they relate harness engineering to context engineering: one distinguishes prompt engineering as changing instructions, context engineering as changing the evidence presented, and harness engineering as treating the operating environment as the object of analysis, while the other describes the harness's layered model boundary as a structured form of context engineering.

## Agentic harnesses: loops, multi-agent orchestration, and agentic RAG

The agent-loop survey states that every LLM agent, beneath its framework and its prompts, runs a loop in which it observes, decides, acts, and observes again until it stops [R8]. The Code as Agent Harness survey says that, rather than exposing the model to a flat list of tools, recent systems wrap it in a managed development loop that controls repository access, file edits, command execution, approval boundaries, context isolation, logging, and validation [R49]. The scaling-the-harness paper holds that agent performance emerges from the interaction among the foundation model, memory substrate, context constructor, skill-routing layer, orchestration loop, and verification-and-governance layer [R26]. The externalization review states that in mature agent systems, reliability increasingly depends on external memory stores, tool registries, protocol definitions, sandboxes, sub-agent orchestration, compression pipelines, evaluators, test harnesses, and approval loops [R87]. These accounts converge on loops, memory, tool use and orchestration as harness components, though each draws the boundary in its own vocabulary.

The Agentic RAG survey says Agentic RAG transcends the limitations of traditional RAG by embedding autonomous AI agents into the RAG pipeline [R17]. The same survey concludes that no single architecture is universally best [R17]. The RAG-stack review reports that agentic RAG combines agents with RAG pipelines for decision-making, self-optimization and multi-agent coordination [R67].

For multi-agent orchestration, the TDD governance paper has the orchestration engine retain authority over state while models only propose [R52]. The MemTool paper offers autonomous, workflow and hybrid modes, showing that the degree of agent autonomy is a design variable [R35]. The Agentic RAG survey lists multi-agent coordination, process-aware evaluation and governance among the open challenges [R17].

## Component attribution across stages: retrieval, memory, tool use, and evaluation

The next step is to compare how the accounts bound the harness and which components each one places inside it. The Code as Agent Harness survey says that prompt engineering changes instructions and context engineering changes what evidence is presented to the model, whereas Agentic Harness Engineering treats the operating environment itself as the object of analysis [R49]. The scaling-the-harness paper argues that agent performance emerges from the interaction among the foundation model, memory substrate, context constructor, skill-routing layer, orchestration loop, and verification-and-governance layer [R26]. The accounts of the harness and its surrounding configuration do not use a single list of components: one names a model, memory substrate, context constructor, skill-routing layer, orchestration loop, and verification-and-governance layer as interacting parts, while another frames the operating environment itself as the object of analysis, distinct from prompt and context engineering. On evaluation, UniACE argues that a reported score reflects a complete model–harness–environment configuration rather than the model alone [R84]. The harness-aware evaluation survey reports that changing the execution system around a fixed model can alter performance and even reverse model rankings [R6]. UniACE's comparisons with source implementations likewise found model-ranking reversals [R84]. The scaling-the-harness paper proposes that future agent benchmarks should additionally measure repeated-use properties such as memory retrieval precision and memory hygiene, and minimal-context efficiency [R26]. The Code as Agent Harness survey cautions that a new retrieval policy may improve benchmark accuracy while increasing hallucinated evidence [R49]. The sources agree that harness-level evaluation must look beyond final task success, but they do not settle how its components should be measured.

## Boundary disputes and evidence limitations

The following paragraphs compare how the terms are bounded and where the accounts diverge. The Agentic Harness Engineering account states that prompt engineering changes instructions and context engineering changes what evidence is presented to the model, whereas harness engineering treats the operating environment itself as the object of analysis [R49]. The prompt and context engineering survey describes a shift from input-level linguistic control to system-level contextual orchestration [R2]. One account separates the terms by object of change: prompt engineering changes instructions, context engineering changes what evidence the model sees, and harness engineering takes the operating environment as its object of analysis. Another survey describes a shift from input-level linguistic control to system-level contextual orchestration. Together these accounts treat the harness as extending the scope of earlier practices, from instructions and evidence toward the wider system around the model. The externalization review also says that a common account of why memory, skills and protocols converge, and of how that reshapes the definition of an agent, remains underdeveloped [R87]. On evidence, the harness-aware evaluation survey reports that changing the execution system around a fixed model can alter performance and even reverse model rankings [R6]. The same survey says that inconsistent reporting of harness configurations, resource budgets, and scoring procedures makes benchmark results difficult to interpret and compare [R6]. The context rot study states that no published empirical study has directly tested the joint hypothesis linking codebase scale, context growth and MCP tool attrition [R20]. The Skills Architecture paper reports consistent results across three model families, while stating that it does not claim to have isolated architecture as the sole cause [R98]. The TDD governance paper likewise calls its empirical validation preliminary and says broader cross-model and repository-scale evaluations are needed [R52].

## Discussion

*Interpretation: VERA's reading of the claims named after each statement, judged to follow from them; not checked against quotes.*

Prompt engineering is defined consistently as designing instructions to guide model output without altering model parameters, and one source adds that prompts can also be seen as a form of programming (C1, C2, C3, C10). The prompt-engineering field is nonetheless described as having conflicting terminology and a fragmented understanding of what makes a prompt effective, and practitioners' skill is called loosely defined, so the consistent definition coexists with an unsettled vocabulary (C12, C14, C1). Context engineering is framed in two compatible ways: as a shift from input-level linguistic control to system-level orchestration, and as adapting inputs with instructions, strategies or evidence rather than weight updates (C51, C18, C16). Several claims attach concrete concerns to context: exceeding the usable window forces decisions about discarding history, and context growth is linked to degraded reasoning and tool-calling reliability (C4, C22). The harness is described as the external execution support structure built around the model, treated as a first-class object of design, evaluation, and optimization. The Code as Agent Harness survey draws the boundary with earlier stages by saying that prompt engineering changes instructions and context engineering changes what evidence is presented, whereas harness engineering treats the operating environment itself as the object of analysis (C25, C7, C43). The claims place the stages relative to one another in different ways. The externalization review calls the harness's separation of the prompt into layers a structured form of context engineering, which places the harness partly inside context engineering. The Code as Agent Harness survey instead presents harness engineering as a separate stage whose object of analysis is the operating environment. The harness-engineering survey describes a boundary that shifts as routine capability-bearing support migrates into model weights while constraint-bearing governance functions stay external (C20, C43, C32). Components attributed to the harness layer include memory, a context constructor, skill routing, an orchestration loop and verification and governance, as well as sandboxes, evaluators and approval loops (C26, C36, C34). The evaluation claims matter because changing the execution system around a fixed model can alter performance and reverse rankings, so a score reflects a model–harness–environment configuration rather than the model alone (C6, C45, C47).

## Conclusions

*Interpretation: VERA's reading of the claims named after each statement, judged to follow from them; not checked against quotes.*

The claims support a layered account in which prompt engineering concerns instructions, context engineering concerns what evidence and history the model sees, and harness engineering concerns the surrounding execution environment (C43, C1, C16). Retrieval, memory, tool use, orchestration and evaluation or verification loops are attributed mostly to the harness and agentic layers, though the claims do not assign each component to one stage exclusively (C26, C36, C5, C34). Boundaries between context engineering and harness engineering are not settled, since the claims offer both a nesting view and a separation view (C20, C43). The claims do not show that any one architecture is best, and they leave open a common account of why memory, skills and protocols converge (C38, C52).

## A research agenda

*Interpretation: VERA's reading of the claims named after each statement, judged to follow from them; not checked against quotes.*

Compare the competing boundary definitions on a shared corpus to test whether prompt, context and harness engineering can be separated consistently (C12, C20, C43). Evaluate agents with harness configurations, budgets and scoring procedures reported in standardised form and with the model held fixed, to measure how much rankings depend on the harness (C54, C6, C45). Extend context-management comparisons beyond synthetic, single-model, single-budget settings, and test them on realistic agent tasks (C24, C23, C4). Directly test the joint link between codebase scale, context growth and tool attrition, which is stated to lack published empirical tests (C55, C22). Run cross-model and repository-scale validations of governance and architecture claims, since existing validation is preliminary or does not isolate causes (C57, C56). Add repeated-use measures such as memory retrieval precision, memory hygiene and minimal-context efficiency to agent benchmarks (C48).

## Threats to validity

Coverage. The paper rests on what 12 queries found; the search was not repeated for the paper. A finding reported as missing means these searches did not find it, not that it does not exist.

Depth of reading. 31 of 46 kept papers were read at the abstract only, so their methods and caveats are known only as their abstracts state them.

What the checks cover. Each claim was checked against the passage it quotes, not against the whole source or the wider literature. A claim can be supported by its quote and still leave out a qualification stated elsewhere in the paper it cites. Interpretation sections were judged to follow from the claims they name; they were not checked against the sources.

Same-family grading. The writer is a Claude model; the judges are a different model family, and any independent scoring of this paper by a Claude model shares the writer's family.
