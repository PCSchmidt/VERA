# From Prompts to Harnesses: How Prompt, Context, and Agentic Harness Engineering Are Defined and Bounded, and Which Technical Components Each Stage Is Credited With

## Abstract

This review traces how prompt engineering, context engineering, harnesses, and agentic harnesses have been defined and bounded in recent literature, and which technical components each stage is credited with. Prompt engineering is described as using task-specific instructions to improve model efficacy without changing core parameters, and as a form of programming, though one survey notes it is often an ad hoc practice lacking a standardized theoretical framework. Context engineering is characterised as modifying inputs with instructions, strategies, or evidence rather than weight updates. One source separates the stages cleanly: prompts change instructions, context changes the evidence presented, and the harness treats the operating environment as the object of analysis. Components are attributed unevenly. Retrieval is tied to prompting, agentic RAG adds reflection, planning, tool use, and multi-agent collaboration, and harness accounts list memory, context construction, skill routing, orchestration, and verification and governance. Several sources agree that the execution system affects measured results, including model-ranking reversals. Context-window degradation motivates context management. No single agreed component-to-stage mapping emerges, the size of harness effects is not established, and whether capabilities migrate into weights remains theorised. Open work includes measuring memory quality, controlled model-versus-harness studies, broader replication, and multi-agent evaluation.

## Introduction

The survey of prompt engineering by R1 describes it as a technique that uses task-specific instructions to enhance model efficacy without modifying core model parameters [R1]. The abstract of R11 states that prompts are also a form of programming that can customize the outputs and interactions with an LLM [R11]. The authors of R2 report that prompt engineering is frequently treated as an empirical, ad hoc practice lacking a standardized theoretical framework [R2]. The same survey frames its analysis around a shift from input-level linguistic control to system-level contextual orchestration [R2]. R1 describes retrieval-augmented generation as emerging as a novel solution that seamlessly weaves information retrieval into the prompting process [R1]. The Agentic RAG survey R17 states that agents leverage reflection, planning, tool use, and multi-agent collaboration to manage retrieval strategies [R17]. The authors of R24 implement memory not as a vector database, but as a persistent file system accessible via tool calls [R24]. The study R18 notes that agents on long-running tasks inevitably exceed their usable context window, forcing a choice about what history to discard [R18]. The paper R20 describes context rot as the progressive and nonlinear degradation of agent reasoning quality and tool calling reliability as the context window fills [R20]. The discussion now moves to the execution layer around the model, which is often called the harness. The authors of R6 state that changing the execution system around a fixed model can alter performance and even reverse model rankings [R6]. The authors of R26 define scaling the harness as treating the structured execution layer around a foundation model as a first-class object of design, evaluation, and optimization [R26]. The survey R8 takes the agent loop, rather than the model in isolation, as its unit of analysis [R8]. Sources describe prompts as task-specific instructions that steer a model without changing its parameters and as a form of programming that customizes LLM outputs, while another survey frames its analysis around a shift from input-level linguistic control to system-level contextual orchestration, and another treats the execution system around a model as a factor that can change performance.

## Method

The question this paper answers was proposed by VERA from the user's topic and confirmed by the user: Between 2025 and the present, how have the definitions, scope, and claimed boundaries of prompt engineering, context engineering, harnesses, and agentic harnesses been articulated in the academic and practitioner literature, and which technical components (e.g., retrieval, memory, tool use, orchestration, evaluation loops) were attributed to each stage?

Search. 12 queries were sent to the paper-search services: “prompt engineering to context engineering definition”; “context engineering for large language model agents”; “agent harness design for LLM agents”; “agentic harness orchestration tool use memory”; “survey of context engineering techniques”; “retrieval-augmented generation and agent memory architectures”; “in-context learning and prompt design foundations”; “LLM agent scaffolding and tool-calling frameworks”; “benchmarks for evaluating LLM agent harnesses”; “long-context limitations context rot degradation”; “failure modes of autonomous LLM agents”; “survey of prompt engineering methods taxonomy”. They retrieved 120 candidate papers. A relevance screen, judged on titles and abstracts, kept 46 and set aside 74.

Reading. Every kept paper was read at its abstract; 15 were also read in full text, and 31 at the abstract only. This gave 195 evidence passages, the only text the paper may quote.

Writing and checking. The evidence sections were drafted sentence by sentence; every sentence that attributes something to a source carries a quote copied from one of those passages. Each quote was checked to be verbatim in its source, and a judge other than the writer checked that the passage supports the sentence and that the quote alone states it; sentences that failed were repaired once or removed. 51 claims from 20 sources survived, resting on 45 distinct quotes (a quote used by more than one sentence is one piece of evidence). Sentences without a claim were checked too: a synthesis names the claims it rests on and was judged to follow from them; any other sentence was judged to state no fact. Interpretation sections, where present, rest only on those claims and are labelled as such.

Models. Written by anthropic/claude-sonnet-5.5; judged by z-ai/glm-5.3-flash.

## Context engineering: moving from prompt text to system-level context

The next question is how the move from crafting prompt text to managing the information a model receives has been described. A survey of prompt and context engineering analyses the shift from input-level linguistic control to system-level contextual orchestration and names context dilution and computational overhead as persistent limitations [R2]. The ACE paper describes LLM applications as increasingly relying on context adaptation, meaning the modification of inputs with instructions, strategies, or evidence rather than weight updates [R94]. The ACE authors report that it optimizes contexts both offline (e.g., system prompts) and online (e.g., agent memory), outperforming strong baselines by +10.6% on agents and +8.6% on finance [R94].

One survey describes a shift from input-level linguistic control to system-level contextual orchestration, and another source distinguishes prompt engineering as changing instructions from context engineering as changing what evidence is presented to the model. Together they frame context engineering as a move beyond the wording of prompts toward the information a model receives. The Code as Agent Harness survey distinguishes prompt engineering as changing instructions and context engineering as changing what evidence is presented to the model, while Agentic Harness Engineering treats the operating environment itself as the object of analysis [R49]. The scaling-the-harness paper lists a context constructor as one of six interacting components alongside a memory store, skill routing, an orchestration loop, and a governance layer [R26].

The following discussion turns to how context engineering relates to the larger harness in which it may be situated. One study states that context rot manifests well before nominal token limits are reached, as tool schema definitions consume a disproportionate share of the context budget and model attention weakens for information deep within growing prompts [R20]. Another reports that, in calibrated simulation over the Agentic Context Benchmark (ACB-2026), Skills Architecture reduced normalized context entropy by 42% relative to baseline MCP [R98].

## Harness engineering: the structure around a fixed model

The harness survey R29 describes the agent harness as the external execution support structure built around the model and treats it as a distinct performance lever [R29]. The scaling-the-harness paper names six interacting components: a reasoning substrate, a memory store, a context constructor, a skill-routing layer, an orchestration loop, and a verification and governance layer [R26]. The externalization review assigns roles differently: memory externalizes state across time, skills externalize procedural expertise, protocols externalize interaction structure, and the harness unifies them into governed execution [R87].

R29 describes a shifting boundary in which routine capability-bearing support migrates into model weights while constraint-bearing governance functions remain external [R29].

## Agentic harnesses: loops, multi-agent orchestration, and agentic RAG

The agent loop survey and the scaling-the-harness paper describe the agent in terms of a loop and the components built around it, and the externalization review places the harness as persistent infrastructure beyond prompt management. The Agent Loop survey states that every LLM agent, beneath its framework and prompts, runs a loop in which it observes, decides, acts, and observes again until it stops [R8]. The same survey theorises that the loop is being internalized into weights and externalized into skills and harnesses [R8]. The scaling-the-harness paper names six components of an agent: a reasoning substrate, memory store, context constructor, skill-routing layer, orchestration loop, and verification and governance layer [R26]. The externalization review places the harness as the stage where capability extends beyond prompt management into persistent infrastructure [R87]. The code-harness survey describes planning as a control layer that decomposes tasks and coordinates multi-stage software workflows [R49].

On multi-agent orchestration, the code-harness survey reports that Anthropic's long-running harnesses separate planning, generation, and evaluation into distinct roles [R49]. The TDD governance paper describes an orchestration engine that retains sole authority over workspace mutation [R52]. For agentic RAG, the survey by its authors concludes that no single architecture is universally optimal and that effectiveness depends on aligning complexity with task requirements [R17]. The RAG-stack review says agentic and AutoRAG architectures combine AI agents with RAG pipelines for dynamic decision-making, self-optimization, and multi-agent coordination [R67].

The code-harness survey lists evaluation beyond final task success and consistent shared state across multiple agents among its open challenges [R49]. The agentic RAG survey likewise lists multi-agent coordination, process-aware evaluation, and governance as unresolved [R17].

## Evaluation loops and the model–harness–environment configuration

Two sources agree that an agent benchmark result reflects more than the model: one states that a reported score reflects a complete model–harness–environment configuration rather than the model alone, and the other reports that changing the execution system around a fixed model can alter performance and even reverse model rankings. The UniACE authors state that a reported score reflects a complete model–harness–environment configuration rather than the model alone [R84]. The harness-aware evaluation survey likewise reports that changing the execution system around a fixed model can alter performance and even reverse model rankings [R6]. The Harness-Aware Evaluation survey reviews the development of agent harnesses and introduces a four-factor evaluation framework comprising the model, harness, environment, and evaluator, alongside the protocol under which they are tested [R6].

Failure analysis and post-training form a second strand. UniACE reports that failure profiles are not only model-dependent but also task-dependent [R84]. The harness–post-training study observes that high ALFWorld success largely stems from harness-aware post-training in which a specific harness is fixed a priori [R42]. The coevolution survey describes a shifting boundary in which routine capability-bearing support migrates into model weights while constraint-bearing governance functions remain external [R29].

Proposals for safety and longitudinal evaluation extend these points. The system-scaling paper argues that agent quality should be evaluated as a longitudinal systems property rather than a one-shot completion score [R26]. The Code as Agent Harness paper holds that a harness mutation should be treated like a code change to a safety-critical runtime [R49]. The externalization review observes that most evaluation logic today lives inside the model's chain of thought or in external benchmark harnesses that run post hoc [R87].

## Contested boundaries and evidence limitations

The following sections turn to how the boundaries between these stages are drawn and how the sources qualify their own evidence. The agentic harness survey states that prompt engineering changes instructions and context engineering changes what evidence is presented to the model, while its harness-level view treats the operating environment as the object of analysis [R49]. The Agentic Context Engineering paper defines context adaptation as modifying inputs with instructions, strategies, or evidence rather than weight updates [R94]. The oncology commentary frames context engineering as embedding models with the why behind institutional practices, clinician experience, and patient-specific nuances [R105]. The three sources therefore use context engineering in different registers, one as evidence presented to a model, one as evolving inputs, and one as domain-specific grounding. A second theme concerns whether harness effects can be separated from model effects. The harness-aware evaluation survey reports that changing the execution system around a fixed model can alter performance and even reverse model rankings [R6]. UniACE reports large bidirectional score changes and model-ranking reversals when compared with source implementations [R84]. The code-as-harness survey lists separating improvements in the harness from improvements in the base model as part of a research agenda [R49]. The discussion now turns to the evidence on individual components. The context-management study acknowledges the limitations of its synthetic, single-model, single-budget setup [R18]. The system-scaling paper notes that single-episode success still dominates reported metrics, leaving memory quality and context efficiency largely unmeasured [R26]. Taken together, these sources suggest that comparative evidence about performance is sensitive to evaluation design: one survey reports that changing the execution system around a fixed model can alter performance and even reverse model rankings, and another reports large bidirectional score changes and ranking reversals relative to source implementations. Another survey lists separating harness improvements from base-model improvements as a research agenda item.

## Discussion

*Interpretation: VERA's reading of the claims named after each statement, judged to follow from them; not checked against quotes.*

Prompt engineering is described as using task-specific instructions to improve model efficacy without changing core model parameters, and as a form of programming that can customize LLM outputs and interactions (C1, C2). One survey reports that prompt engineering is often treated as an empirical, ad hoc practice without a standardized theoretical framework, and frames the field as moving from input-level linguistic control to system-level contextual orchestration (C3, C4). Context engineering is characterised as context adaptation, meaning modification of inputs with instructions, strategies, or evidence rather than weight updates, and one clinical commentary frames it as embedding the reasons behind institutional practice and patient-specific nuance (C14, C45, C46). The claims draw the boundary between stages consistently in one source: prompt engineering changes instructions, context engineering changes the evidence presented, and the harness-level view treats the operating environment as the object of analysis (C16, C44). Components are attributed unevenly across stages: retrieval is tied to prompting through retrieval-augmented generation woven into the prompting process, while agentic RAG is described as having agents use reflection, planning, tool use, and multi-agent collaboration to manage retrieval; in one study, memory is implemented as a persistent file system accessed via tool calls rather than as a vector database (C5, C6, C7). At the harness stage, sources list components such as memory store, context constructor, skill routing, orchestration loop, and verification and governance layer, and one review assigns memory, skills, and protocols distinct externalizing roles unified by the harness (C21, C22, C17). Two sources describe a shifting boundary between model and external structure, but frame it differently: one says routine capability-bearing support migrates into weights while constraint-bearing governance stays external, and another says the loop is being internalized into weights and externalized into skills and harnesses (C23, C25). Several sources agree that the surrounding execution system matters for measured results: changing it around a fixed model can alter performance and even reverse model rankings, a reported score reflects a complete model–harness–environment configuration rather than the model alone, and UniACE reports large bidirectional score changes and ranking reversals relative to source implementations (C10, C35, C48). Context-window degradation is reported as a persistent constraint motivating context management: usable windows are exceeded on long tasks, and context rot degrades reasoning and tool calling (C8, C9, C18).

## Conclusions

*Interpretation: VERA's reading of the claims named after each statement, judged to follow from them; not checked against quotes.*

The claims support a reading in which the literature moves from instruction-level control to input and evidence management and then to the surrounding execution environment, though the boundaries are articulated differently across sources (C4, C16, C20). Retrieval, memory, tool use, orchestration, and evaluation or governance all appear in the literature, but the claims do not show a single agreed mapping of each component to one stage (C5, C6, C7, C21, C29). Where evaluation is concerned, the claims support that harness choice affects reported outcomes, but they do not establish how large this effect is in general (C36, C48). Whether capabilities will migrate into weights or remain external is a theorised boundary, not a settled finding (C23, C25).

## A research agenda

*Interpretation: VERA's reading of the claims named after each statement, judged to follow from them; not checked against quotes.*

Sources differ in how they list components and place stage boundaries: one distinguishes prompt engineering (instructions) from context engineering (evidence presented) and harness engineering (operating environment), another lists six harness components, and another assigns memory, skills, protocols, and harness distinct externalization roles. Prompt engineering is also described as lacking a standardized theoretical framework, so a shared coding scheme for components would be needed to compare them (C16, C21, C22, C3). Measure memory quality and context efficiency alongside single-episode success, because these are described as largely unmeasured (C51, C41). Run controlled studies that hold the model fixed and vary the harness, and the reverse, to separate harness gains from base-model gains (C49, C10, C39). Replicate context-management findings beyond synthetic, single-model, single-budget settings (C50, C8). Develop process-aware evaluation and shared-state consistency tests for multi-agent systems, since these are listed as open (C33, C34).

## Threats to validity

Coverage. The paper rests on what 12 queries found; the search was not repeated for the paper. A finding reported as missing means these searches did not find it, not that it does not exist.

Depth of reading. 31 of 46 kept papers were read at the abstract only, so their methods and caveats are known only as their abstracts state them.

What the checks cover. Each claim was checked against the passage it quotes, not against the whole source or the wider literature. A claim can be supported by its quote and still leave out a qualification stated elsewhere in the paper it cites. Interpretation sections were judged to follow from the claims they name; they were not checked against the sources.

Same-family grading. The writer is a Claude model; the judges are a different model family, and any independent scoring of this paper by a Claude model shares the writer's family.
